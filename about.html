A live picture of how money and the real economy are moving together — cash, rates, growth, inflation, and fear — and what that mix usually means for bonds, credit, and risk assets.

The idea

Read left to right. Cheap or scarce cash shows up first. Rates and the dollar pass that pressure around. Growth and inflation say what households and firms are actually doing. Risk tells you whether markets are calm or stressed. When you know that mix, you can ask the useful question: so what for assets?

The five lights

Each colored light is a short status for one part of the machine — Liquidity, Rates, Growth, Inflation, or Risk. Behind every light is a handful of related public indicators. Together they decide whether that part of the machine looks easy, mixed, or tight.

  • Tap a light — the table jumps to the indicators that set it, highlighted in blue.
  • Tap “Tap for who voted” — see those indicators, their scores, and a short plain-language read.
  • 1m · 3m · 6m · 1y — the turn, not “is it high.” Lights stay on the economic level. Six months is the default.

All five lights share one axis, and it is worth learning before anything else on this page. Green is the reflationary end — more cash, easier funding, firmer growth, hotter prices, more appetite for risk. Red is the contractionary end — the same five things going the other way. Neither colour means good or bad. A green Inflation light is not the app approving of inflation; it is telling you prices are running hot, which is usually bad for long bonds and fine for commodities. Read the colours as a direction the whole machine is leaning, then let the asset classes below tell you who that helps.

Green — reflationary: easier, stronger, hotter, risk-on Amber — mixed, or near the middle Red — contractionary: tighter, softer, colder, risk-off

So what

Under the five lights sit six asset classes — cash, Treasuries (5 / 10 / 30), credit, equities, gold, commodities. Green, amber, and red mean in, mixed, and out of favor. Tap that row for the full So what: duration, credit, the curve and IG/HY split, confirm, and what would change the call. Crypto follows equities unless it disagrees.

Duration risk

How painful rising rates and sticky inflation are for long bonds and other assets that get paid far in the future. Rising means discount rates are biting harder.

Credit risk

How worried markets are that borrowers still pay. Built from growth, fear gauges, credit spreads, and whether bank lending is speeding up or slowing down.

In / out of favor

The same mix, mapped onto six asset classes. Cash is bills (you get paid to wait when rates are high). Treasuries split 5s, 10s, and 30s. Credit is one class; IG vs HY is in the tap. Equities include the crypto footnote. Gold is money/fear; commodities are the real cycle.

Confirm

Extra checks that line up with the story — or don’t — such as how stocks and bonds are moving together, the dollar, gold, or bitcoin versus cash conditions.

Falsify

The clean flip that would undo the read. Example: inflation turns cold while growth stays strong softens a “duration is hard” call.

The mapping

Lights are where it is versus an economic line — 7% inflation is high even if it just fell. The 1m / 3m / 6m / 1y row is which way it’s going. The six asset classes use both. GDP is quarterly (1m and 3m mean the latest quarter). Liquidity color is scarce vs ample, not the dollar stock; the clocks show adding vs draining.

From the lights we score two risks. Duration asks whether long coupons are taxed (hot inflation, tight real funding) — cooling this window can soften that even when the level is still high. Credit asks whether borrowers still look good for the money. Stocks vs bonds follows that pair.

  • Cash — in when rates, plumbing, or fear are tight. High rates pay you to wait in bills. Out only when cash, rates, and fear are all easy.
  • Treasuries — 5s follow policy, 10s follow duration, 30s follow inflation. A flight to quality can help 10s and 30s if inflation isn’t hot.
  • Credit — one color. IG cares about duration as well as spreads; HY cares about growth and fear. If they disagree, the block is mixed.
  • Equities — in when growth is firm and fear isn’t in charge. Soft growth, draining cash, or risk-off takes them out. Crypto follows equities unless it disagrees with the cash story.
  • Gold — in for hot prices, fear, or a cash drain that isn’t a rates squeeze. Cold inflation, risk-on, and high funding usually leave it out.
  • Commodities — in when growth is firm and inflation is hot (copper, oil). Soft growth or cold prices take the real cycle out.

What happened last time

A read is worth more when you know how often it has been right. Under So what sits a base rate: the app scores every trading day back to 2018 with the same five lights, finds the days that sat closest to today, and shows what the market actually did over the weeks that followed — the median move and how often each asset rose.

Three things to hold in mind. It says how close the match is, and a distant match is context rather than evidence. Days sitting next to each other in the calendar are nearly the same day, so matches are kept at least a month apart to stop one episode counting as twenty. And the replay scores the economic indicators using figures that have since been revised, so it is kinder than the numbers a trader actually had that morning; the market-priced gauges — funding, the curve, volatility, spreads — are not revised and do not have that problem.

It is a base rate, not a forecast. Its most useful moments are when it disagrees with the read above it, because that tells you the call rests on this cycle being different from the last few.

How to read it

  1. Pick the clock — 1m, 3m, 6m, or 1y for the turn.
  2. Read the lights — cash → rates → growth → inflation → fear.
  3. Read the asset classes — what that mix usually favors, including 5 / 10 / 30.
  4. Open a light — the indicators behind it, and the short read.
  5. Tap the asset classes — duration, credit, in/out, confirm, falsify.
  6. Glance at Markets — last prints on open; stats from the daily pull.

Glossary

Reading the numbers

Level
Where a light sits versus an economic line (2% inflation, ~4% unemployment, VIX/HY bands). High is high. Not a table column — only a few series vote this.
Impulse
Up, flat, or down over 1m, 3m, 6m, or 1y. The turn. Monthly series: 1m is the last print vs the one before.
1m · 3m · 6m · 1y
Impulse clocks. Default 6m. Does not recolor the lights.

In the app

Lights
The five status colors for liquidity, rates, growth, inflation, and risk. Tap one to see the indicators behind it.
Markets
Stocks, commodities, crypto, gold, and related prices — how risk assets are behaving, separate from the five lights.
Last print
The newest market price shown when you open Markets. Age sits beside the Markets title; the circle refreshes. Listed prices can lag about 15 minutes. The data table shows Latest; Chart view is the turn.
So what
The six asset classes under the lights, and the panel behind a tap: duration risk, credit risk, 5/10/30, IG vs HY, what confirms, and what would falsify.
The mapping
Usual in/out from the lights: level first, then the impulse clock. Duration and credit, then cash, the Treasury curve, credit, equities, gold, commodities.
Duration risk
How rate moves change the value of cash flows that arrive later (bonds, and stock valuations that lean on the far future).
Credit risk
Doubt that cash flows arrive at all — spreads, growth, and risk appetite.
Credit impulse
Whether bank lending growth is speeding up or slowing down. Central-bank plumbing is one cash story; private lending is the other.
Nominal − real GDP
Nominal growth minus real growth — how much of the expansion is prices versus actual activity.

Plumbing & rates

Asset (central bank)
Something the central bank owns — usually bonds, sometimes ETFs or loans.
Reserves
Cash banks hold at the Fed. Fuel for money markets.
TGA
Treasury General Account — the government’s checking account at the Fed. When it fills, cash leaves the banking system.
ON RRP
Overnight reverse repo — cash parked at the Fed overnight. When it rises, less cash sits in markets.
Net liquidity
Fed assets minus TGA minus ON RRP. Rising usually means more cash in markets.
Real yield
Yield after inflation (TIPS). Higher real yields tighten conditions for risk assets.
Breakeven
The inflation rate priced into markets from nominal bonds versus TIPS.
Curve (2s10s)
Long yield minus short yield. Negative (inverted) often warns of a slowdown.

Fear & credit

OAS
Option-adjusted spread — extra yield a corporate bond pays over Treasuries. Wider means more credit stress.
VIX / MOVE
Implied volatility — equity fear (VIX) and Treasury fear (MOVE).
