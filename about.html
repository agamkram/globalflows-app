GlobalFlows

Every morning the public book is turned into two things. First, five lights — Liquidity, Rates, Growth, Inflation, Risk — that say where the world is on each of those questions. Second, six asset classes — Treasuries, Credit, Equities, Crypto, Gold, Commodity — graded in favor, mixed, or out of favor from what that mix has historically rewarded. Same two-step a macro desk runs: describe the regime, then say what the regime pays for.

The idea

Read left to right. That is the order money actually travels: cash conditions show up first, rates and the dollar pass the pressure around, growth and inflation say what households and firms are doing, and risk tells you whether markets have noticed yet. When you know that mix, you can ask the useful question: so what for assets?

Where the numbers come from

Public sources: FRED, the New York Fed, Yahoo Finance, Bundesbank, Bank of England, Japan’s Ministry of Finance, and S&P earnings yield from multpl. A morning job at 8:47 New York time — after the 8:30 prints, before the open — pulls the book, rebuilds the archive from 2003, refits the calibration, bakes today’s regime, then runs the sanity check. If the check fails, the bake does not publish.

Market prices move every day; payrolls arrive monthly; GDP and China’s credit stock arrive quarterly and late. A series that has gone quiet past its allowed age stops voting and disappears from the table rather than casting a stale opinion. On open, last prints that can move refresh again. Weekly, monthly, and quarterly lines stay on the last official release. Analog history stays the morning snapshot.

Some lines nobody publishes, so the app builds them — net liquidity, funding versus the policy ceiling, reserves and net liquidity as a share of GDP, G4 balance sheets in dollars, the equity risk premium, credit impulse. Each identity is in The math.

The five lights

Each light answers one question: is cash cheap or scarce, is the price of money easy or tight, is real activity strong or soft, is price pressure hot or cold, is the market calm or frightened. Behind every light is a handful of related public indicators, grouped so cousins do not get counted twice.

  • Chevron — ▲ up, ▼ down, – flat. Which way the light has moved over the lookback you picked. This is the turn, not the level.
  • Word — the plain reading. Each light has its own three words: Liquidity Easing / Neutral / Tightening; Rates Easy / Neutral / Tight; Growth Strong / Mid / Soft; Inflation Hot / Mid / Cold; Risk Risk-on / Neutral / Risk-off.
  • Score and needle — a number from −1.5 to +1.5, drawn on a rail with ticks at ±0.45, where the colour changes. Positive is the reflationary end. If the light is close to flipping, the score line says so. If the indicators behind it disagree sharply, the label turns white — a split, and a warning that the single word on the front is hiding an argument.

All five lights share one axis. Green is the reflationary end — more cash, easier funding, firmer growth, hotter prices, more appetite for risk. Red is the contractionary end — the same five things going the other way. Amber is mixed or near the middle. Neither colour means good or bad. A green Inflation light is Hot: prices are running, not the app approving. That is the one trap. The same three colours do a second job on the six asset classes, where they mean in favor, mixed, and out of favor.

Green — reflationary: easier, stronger, hotter, risk-on Amber — mixed, or near the middle Red — contractionary: tighter, softer, colder, risk-off

The 1w · 2w · 1m buttons select the window for the turn. One month is the default. The lookback drives the chevrons, the sparklines, the asset-class needles, and the wording of the narrative. It does not recolour the lights. The lights are where things are; the lookback is which way they are heading. A light can be red and turning up — two different facts.

  • Tap a light — the table jumps to the indicators that set it, highlighted in blue.
  • Tap “Tap for who voted” — the roster: every indicator behind the light, its latest print, and a plain-language read of what the light is saying and why.

Inflation votes core PCE, core CPI, and the market’s 5y5y — not household surveys. Risk votes VIX, high-yield and Baa spreads (the long credit history), and NFCI. MOVE stays on Rates: a spike can tighten the light and the turn; calm does not vote easy. How those votes become one number is in The math.

Why “global”

Dollars are made in more than one place. The Fed is the largest single source, but the European Central Bank, the Bank of Japan and China together run balance sheets of comparable size, and the money they create does not stay home — it competes for the same bonds, the same equities and the same credit. A dashboard that watches only the Fed will call liquidity tight while the rest of the world is flooding the system, and be wrong for years at a time.

So three of the voters are deliberately not American:

  • G4 balance sheets, 12-month change — the Fed, ECB, Bank of Japan and China added together in dollars, and measured by how fast the total is growing. A balance-sheet level rises forever and so can never be called high or low; the rate of change is the thing that eases or tightens. Note that flat is not neutral here: world nominal output grows around 5% a year, so balance sheets held still are quietly shrinking against the economy they fund.
  • The dollar, 12-month change — most of the world borrows in a currency it cannot print. When the dollar rises, every one of those borrowers is squeezed at once, no matter what any central bank intends. The dollar is the transmission belt, which is why it votes on Liquidity rather than just sitting on the tape.
  • Global long rates — the average 10-year yield across Germany, the UK and Japan, from official daily prints: Bundesbank Bund term structure, Bank of England gilt par yield, Japan Ministry of Finance JGB. Duration is priced globally: when Bunds and JGBs move, Treasuries follow. A Rates light built only from US yields mistakes a worldwide repricing for an American one. The OECD monthly averages stay on the book as the slow official print.

One honest limit. FRED does not carry the People's Bank of China balance sheet, so the China leg is Chinese FX reserves instead. That is not the same number — but for global dollar liquidity it is arguably the more relevant one, because reserves are the part that actually recycles into world markets. China private credit as a share of GDP does vote Liquidity at half weight (the four-quarter impulse only moves the lookback). Euro area HICP and euro/Japan unemployment sit on the street and do not vote. The Growth and Inflation lights are still US voters. Japan monthly CPI is not on a live public pipe we can keep — OECD-on-FRED died in 2021 — so it is not faked.

The six asset classes

Under the five lights sits a strip of six cells: Treasuries (5 / 10 / 30), Credit (IG / HY), Equities (Cy / Df), Crypto, Gold, Commodity (Oi / Cu). Green, amber, and red mean in, mixed, and out of favor. The needle shows lean within the call. It cannot flip the call. A Treasuries cell that is red with the needle nudging right means “out of favor, but less emphatically than yesterday” — not “nearly in favor.”

Crypto is Bitcoin. Gold stands alone, as money and as fear. Bills are not on this strip — the front end is the Rates light’s business.

Duration risk

Will owning long bonds hurt? Rising duration risk comes from inflation that is hot and not cooling, tight funding, and a compressed term premium. Falling duration risk comes from cold inflation, easing funding, a wide term premium, and high real yields — whether you are actually paid to take duration, as opposed to merely being able to.

Credit risk

Will borrowers pay? Rising credit risk comes from soft growth, expensive fear, activity rolling over, high-yield spreads widening, and a slowing credit impulse in the US or China. Falling is the mirror.

Those two, crossed, give the headline pairing:

  • Duration risk rising and credit risk falling → stocks over long Treasuries
  • Duration risk falling and credit risk rising → long Treasuries over stocks
  • Both rising, cash paid → cash over stocks and bonds
  • Both falling → risk assets and duration can both work

Each class runs a checklist of conditions. The conditions are about interactions, not levels: not “growth is soft, therefore sell,” but “growth is soft while fear is still cheap” — the combination that has actually hurt. Valuation sits in the checklist both ways, against 23-year medians: term premium and the 10-year real yield for Treasuries, the high-yield spread (or Baa over longer history) for Credit, earnings yield minus the 10-year real for Equities, positioning for Gold. Cheap adds, expensive subtracts.

  • Treasuries — the 5-year is a policy call: is funding easing or tightening. The 10s and 30s are duration risk plus inflation plus whether the term premium and real yields pay you to hold them. The parent call is the long end only. The 5-year is shown on the strip and left out of the call, because the front end is what the Rates light already measures.
  • Credit — investment grade is mostly duration with a credit overlay. High yield is mostly the growth-and-fear question, plus whether spreads pay you. “Spreads are tight — you are not paid” is a standalone out condition. If the two disagree, the Credit title is mixed.
  • Equities — expensive fear opens the risk premium; complacent fear into firm growth is late to the expansion. A wide earnings yield against real yields adds. Cash draining into a still-calm tape subtracts. Cy (cyclicals, XLY) leans harder on growth; Df (defensives, XLP) leans harder on fear.
  • Crypto — Bitcoin, the high-beta valve. Easy plumbing into cheap fear is the classic dump. Drain into paid fear is the bounce sample. A rising dollar and high real yields both tax it. It does not vote a light.
  • Gold — three jobs, no permanent one: crisis plumbing, an inflation wage when the dollar is soft, and deflation fear. When none of those is live, the app says so — “gold has no job right now” — rather than inventing a reason. Crowded speculative longs lean against the call.
  • Commodity — oil and copper are not the same trade. Cu is the industrial growth read; Oi carries the dollar and supply. When they disagree the parent says so rather than averaging the disagreement into silence.

Tap a class for the call, the sentence explaining why, the component split, and what history says. Full regime inside that card is the longer sheet: the story, duration and credit risk, the full in/out list, what happened last time, also-note for tensions, and Watch for what would falsify. That last one is the app naming, in advance, the print that would prove it wrong.

What happened last time

Under each asset class, and in the Full regime sheet, the app finds the days in its 23-year record whose five-light mix most resembles today, and reports what happened next: the median move and how often it went that way. This is a base rate, not a forecast. It says “when the world has looked like this before, here is what followed.”

The app grades its own match: a close match is worth weight, a loose match is context, a distant match is context rather than evidence. It also says whether history agrees with today’s call — History agrees, History disagrees, History is a coin flip, or History leans up/down. When history disagrees, the class title on the strip turns white. That is the app flagging its own internal argument rather than hiding it.

Days sitting next to each other in the calendar are nearly the same day, so matches are kept at least a few weeks apart to stop one episode counting as twenty. Jobs, prices, GDP and NFCI are scored on ALFRED vintages — the print that was public that morning — where the vintage file reaches. Funding, the curve, volatility and spreads are market-priced and not revised.

How to read it

  1. Pick the lookback — 1w, 2w, or 1m for the turn. Lights stay on where it is now.
  2. Read the lights — cash → rates → growth → inflation → fear.
  3. Read the asset classes — what that mix usually favors, including 5 / 10 / 30.
  4. Open a light — the indicators behind it, and the short read.
  5. Tap an asset class — why that call, the split, the analog. Full regime is inside the card.
  6. Glance at Markets — last prints on open; stats from the daily pull.

Everything else on screen

  • The series table — every indicator, latest print coloured by direction over your lookback. Tap a name for what it is, which light it votes, how often it prints, its as-of date, and a link to the source. Tap the value to flip that row into a sparkline.
  • Chart / Values — flips the whole table. Each chart is the lookback window, with the change labelled beside it.
  • Markets shelf — on Markets, buckets appear: All, Equities, Credit, Duration, Metals, Energy, Ag, Crypto. An age stamp shows how old the last prints are. Listed names can lag about 15 minutes.
  • Compare — pick up to 10 series and view them together. Clubs can be saved to three slots (m1, m2, m3) and recalled later.
  • Build mark — the number in the top-right of this page, and the ?v= on the files. If the page ever looks like last week’s, that is the thing to check.

The math

Everything the app computes — rather than copies off FRED or Yahoo — is named here, with the identity used at ingest. Today’s fitted centres load from this morning’s file so the numbers on this page can be checked against the bake, not against a screenshot of last month’s bands.

Derived series

Dollar stocks (Fed assets, loans, a balance sheet) grow with the economy. They never vote a colour. They either sit on the lookback, or they are turned into a ratio, a spread, or a rate of change that can still mean the same thing in ten years. FRED units are a trap: Fed assets and the Treasury account arrive in millions, reverse repo in billions, Bank of Japan assets in hundreds of millions of yen. The identities below are what the ingest actually does with those units.

Net liquidity
WALCL(bn) − TGA(bn) − ON RRP(bn) Fed assets and the Treasury account are millions on FRED, so each is divided by 1,000 first. Reverse repo is already billions. Rising means more cash in markets. The dollar stock does not vote; the GDP share does.
Reserves ÷ GDP
(WRESBAL millions ÷ 1,000) ÷ nominal GDP × 100 Bank reserves as a percent of the economy. Scored against its own trailing five-year normal, not a fixed band — reserves near zero before 2008 and enormous after are not the same system.
Net liquidity ÷ GDP
net liquidity (bn) ÷ nominal GDP × 100 The same plumbing stack as a share of GDP. Also trailing five-year.
Funding vs target top
(overnight funding − fed funds target top) × 100 In basis points. From 2018-04-02 that is SOFR; before then, effective fed funds versus the target, so the funding ballot covers the whole archive. Deeply negative means cash is trading inside the corridor; at or above zero means reserves are scarce. Trailing five-year. High is tight.
G4 central bank assets
Fed + ECB×USD/EUR + BoJ×(100 / yen per dollar) + China FX reserves Each leg converted at that day’s market rate, then summed in trillions of dollars. The China leg is FX reserves, not the PBoC balance sheet. The level does not vote.
Global CB assets, 12m
(G4 today − G4 ~1y ago) / |G4 ~1y ago| × 100 This is the G4 voter. Neutral sits at +5%, not zero, because world nominal output grows around that much. Flat balance sheets are a mild drain.
Dollar, 12m change
(broad dollar today − ~1y ago) / |~1y ago| × 100 DTWEXBGS. Rising is tight for Liquidity: most of the world borrows dollars it cannot print.
Global long rates (G3)
(Bund 10y + gilt 10y + JGB 10y) / 3 Official daily prints, last observation carried across holidays so one country’s close does not drop the average.
Credit impulse (US)
Δ over ~1y of (TOTLL YoY %) The change in the year-over-year growth rate of bank loans, in percentage points. Second derivative: it turns before the stock does. Lookback only — it does not vote a colour.
China credit impulse
China credit/GDP today − China credit/GDP four quarters ago BIS private credit as a share of GDP, via FRED. Lookback only. The stock itself votes Liquidity at half weight because the print lags two to three quarters.
Equity risk premium
S&P earnings yield − 10-year real yield What stocks pay you over inflation-protected government debt. Wide = equities cheap versus duration.
Nominal − real GDP
nominal GDP YoY − real GDP YoY How much of the expansion is prices versus actual activity. Does not vote a light; used in the story, not the ballot.
Stock–bond correlation
60-day Pearson of SPX returns vs −Δ 10-year yield The yield change is sign-flipped so a falling yield counts as a positive bond return. Rising together means less diversification. Confirm/falsify, not a light vote.
Gold speculative positioning
156-week min–max index of CFTC non-commercial net % of open interest 0 is the least crowded print in that window, 100 the most. Raw contract counts drift with open interest, so only the index carries a band. Taxes the Gold call when longs are crowded. Does not vote Risk.
2-year real (Rates vote)
2-year yield − core PCE YoY Built at score time, not stored as its own series. TIPS 5y and 10y vote as published; this is the one trailing-core-PCE construct still on Rates.

How a print becomes a score

Each voter is mapped onto a common −1 to +1. Positive is always the reflationary side of that light — more cash, easier funding, firmer growth, hotter prices, more risk appetite. Two methods:

  • A band — three numbers for that series: low, normal, high, typically the 10th percentile, the median, and the 90th of its own history. Below low is −1; at the middle is 0; above high is +1; in between, a straight line. If high is the contractionary end (VIX, real yields, the dollar), the sign is flipped once, in the band, not again later.
  • Trailing five-year normal — used where a fixed band would lie across a regime change: reserves ÷ GDP, net liquidity ÷ GDP, and the funding spread. Score is how many standard deviations the print sits from its own recent mean, divided by two and clipped to −1…+1. A 2005 reading is judged on 2000–2005, never against a future it could not have known. About two years of history are required before it will vote; short of that, it abstains.

Bands are never guessed. An audit flags any series pinned at the top or bottom of its band more than half the time — a constant wearing the costume of a signal. Reverse repo did that for two years; financial conditions did it for a decade. Both were pulled off the colour vote.

How scores become a light

Related indicators are grouped into ballots, and the ballots are averaged — not the raw indicators. Core CPI and core PCE would otherwise give inflation’s realized side two votes. Inside a ballot, members are weighted (core PCE counts double against core CPI). Across ballots:

  • Liquidity — Fed balance sheet (the two GDP shares) · funding cost · funding stress (commercial paper) · global central banks and the dollar · China credit at half weight.
  • Rates — real yields · nominal yields · the curve · bond volatility.
  • Growth — coincident (jobs, output) at half weight · leading at 1.5 · surveys.
  • Inflation — realized (weight 2) · persistence · expectations.
  • Risk — credit spreads · volatility.

If a light has five or more ballots, the highest and the lowest are dropped before the weighted average, so one wild reading cannot carry it. With three or four ballots the trim is skipped — dropping ends there would throw away the only Strong or Soft votes and pin the light Mid. Liquidity is the light with five seats, so it is the one that trims.

Two overrides where averaging would lie:

  • MOVE can turn Rates red but never green. A spike means duration is unownable. Calm is not cheap money, so silence does not get to vote easy — on the light or on the turn.
  • A broken funding market floors Liquidity. If the funding-cost ballot or the commercial-paper ballot is at or below −0.5, the light is held at least that tight. Quantity ballots cannot talk it back up. In October 2008 reserves were far above normal because the Fed was flooding a market where commercial paper cost 242 basis points over fed funds. Easy funding never floors the light the other way.

Each light’s raw mix is then recentered on that light’s own 23-year average and stretched so all five share one spread, then clipped to −1.5…+1.5:

score = clip( ((raw − mean) / sd) × shared spread × scale, −1.5, +1.5 )

Colour changes at ±0.45. With that line and the shared spread, a well-behaved light is amber roughly 60% of the time. That is arithmetic, not indecision. Today’s fitted mean, sd, and scale for each light:

The lookback, and when a series stops voting

1w is 7 calendar days, 2w is 14, 1m is 30. Monthly series take one step back for anything under about six weeks. Quarterly series share the latest quarter on 1w and 1m. A small deadband treats noise as flat before a chevron is drawn. The lookback score is the signed change over that window; it never recolours the light.

A print older than its allowance does not vote: 14 days for a daily, 21 for a weekly, 85 for a monthly (observation-month dating, so a late release of last month still counts), 190 for a quarterly. Past that, it is hidden as stale.

Duration risk and credit risk

Before any class is graded, two nets are formed. Positive means that risk is falling. Each light score is first turned into a soft 0–1 weight: it starts turning on at −0.2 and is fully on at the colour line (±0.45), so a light sitting at +0.44 still counts almost as much as one that has just flipped.

Duration net (positive = duration risk falling):

−0.70 × hot inflation that is not cooling +0.65 × cold inflation +0.45 × hot-but-cooling this window −0.55 × tight Rates +0.35 × easy Rates +0.70 × term-premium z +0.55 × 10-year real-yield z −0.25 × strong Growth × (1 − cold inflation)

Credit net (positive = credit risk falling):

+0.35 × strong Growth −0.35 × soft Growth +0.35 × Risk-on −0.35 × Risk-off −0.30 if activity is rolling over this window, +0.20 if it is firming −0.30 if high-yield spreads are widening, +0.20 if they are tightening −0.35 if US bank-credit impulse is slowing, +0.35 if it is speeding up −0.15 if China credit impulse is slowing, +0.15 if it is speeding up +0.25 × credit-spread z (wide = you are paid)

Either net at or above +0.35 is falling; at or below −0.35 is rising; in between is mixed.

Valuation z is (print − median) / scale, clipped to −1…+1. Four of the six centres are the median of the same 2003–2026 window they are then applied to, so each reads cheap about half the time as arithmetic. How a class gets in / mixed / out

Each class adds and subtracts weights for the conditions that fire. The total is the net. Most classes map in at +0.35 and out at −0.35. Credit uses a slightly tighter pair; commodities use a looser one, so they lean more often. The 5-year is shown on the Treasuries strip and left out of the parent net. The lookback may slide the needle; it cannot flip the call.

How we know it is right

Nothing in this app has ever broken loudly. Every real bug ran clean, produced a plausible number, and stated something false with total confidence. Four standing checks, plus a forward record. The daily bake will not publish if the first check fails.

  • Sanity — recomputes all eleven boxes (five lights, six classes) independently and confirms they match what was published. Also checks that no series is stale beyond its allowance and that every print is in a plausible range. Today’s result is the “Today’s check” line above.
  • Band audit — flags any band where the series sits pinned at an extreme, or barely moves. A light that reaches one colour on under 8% of days has that colour in name only. A green:red ratio worse than 3:1 usually means a band is tracking an era rather than a condition.
  • Episode audit — lines the five lights up against 24 named events (the GFC, the 2013 taper tantrum, 2018–19 QT, COVID, the 2021–22 inflation surge, and so on), each with an expected reading written from what actually happened. A well-shaped distribution is not the same as being right about a crisis. This is the check that found the largest error the app has had.
  • Call audit — replays the grading logic across the whole archive and asks whether the classes called in favor actually outperformed the ones called out of favor, on independent non-overlapping windows, not on consecutive days that are really one bet. Fifty-eight days graded on a one-month forward return is about two bets on one episode, not fifty-eight.

One episode is worth keeping, because it is the distinction the lights rest on. Rates reads Easy through the 2013 taper tantrum. The 10-year went from 1.6% to 3.0% in four months — that looks wrong until you notice the 2-year sat at 0.2%, the real 5-year never got above zero, QE was still running $85bn a month, and the curve steepened. Rates were easy the whole way; they became dramatically less easy. The drama lives in the turn. The light showed green, falling steadily. A light that went red there would be reporting the turn and calling it the level.

Every number in those backtests is measured over the same history the settings were chosen on. The forward scorecard is the test that cannot flatter the app: each morning the six calls are written down, and later marked once the outcome exists. That record began in September 2026. It prints empty on purpose, and it will not report a hit rate until a class has twelve independent windows. regime-history.json is a replay of today’s model over revised data, rebuilt whenever the model changes. regime-log.json is what a reader actually saw that morning, and is never recomputed.

Honest limits

  • The archive starts in 2003 — the first date where real yields, inflation breakevens, and net liquidity all exist together. It covers the GFC, QE, COVID, and the 2022 tightening. It has never seen the 1970s, and it has never seen Volcker.
  • Where possible the archive uses the print as it was published that morning. For some economic series that vintage record only reaches back to around 2016; before that the app is reading revised figures, which is slightly kinder to it than reality was.
  • Bitcoin’s record starts in 2014. Its base rates should not be read beside a multi-decade Treasury or equity sample as an equal.
  • China’s credit stock lags two to three quarters. It votes at half weight for exactly that reason.
  • “In favor” means this mix has historically rewarded owning this, over about a one-month horizon. It is a base rate, not a target, and not advice.
  • Bands, calibration, valuation medians, and class checklists were fitted across 2003–2026 and then graded across 2003–2026. The episode audit is more independent, because its expectations come from recorded history rather than from the app. The only real test of a macro tool is forward time, and that record has just started.
  • Four of the six valuation centres use the median of the same window they are applied to, so none of them can ever report that a whole era was expensive — the era is inside the centre. High yield, pinned at 4.0%, reads “you are not paid” on most days the app has run, which is true of this period and means half of the credit call is not currently separating one day from another.
  • The conditions behind each class call are economically motivated; the exact weights are judgment, tuned until the audit passed, against maybe five genuinely independent macro cycles. That is a real overfitting risk. What protects it is that the conditions describe interactions a desk would recognise — soft growth into cheap fear, tight spreads that do not pay you — rather than being fitted curves.

Glossary

On the lights

Light
One of the five regime reads: Liquidity, Rates, Growth, Inflation, Risk. Tap one to see the indicators behind it.
Word
The plain-English state of a light — Hot, Soft, Tightening.
Score
The light’s number, −1.5 to +1.5. Colour changes at ±0.45.
Needle
The score drawn on a rail. On an asset class, shows lean within the call — it cannot flip the call.
Chevron
▲▼– beside a light. Which way it has moved over the lookback.
1w · 2w · 1m
Lookback. Default 1m. Sets the turn. Does not recolor the lights.
Ballot
A group of related indicators that vote together, so correlated measures do not get counted twice.
Band
The three numbers — low, normal, high — that convert one series’ print into a comparable score.

In the app

Markets
Stocks, commodities, crypto, gold, and related prices — how risk assets are behaving, separate from the five lights.
Last print
Newest print for series that can move between bakes. Age sits by the street title; the circle refreshes. Listed names can lag about 15 minutes. Chart uses the same last bar. Weekly/monthly/quarterly stay on the morning bake.
So what
The six asset classes under the lights, and the panel behind a tap: duration risk, credit risk, 5/10/30, IG/HY, Cy/Df, Oi/Cu on the strip, what confirms, and what would falsify.
In / mixed / out of favor
The asset-class call. Green, amber, red on the strip.
Duration risk
Whether owning long bonds is likely to hurt.
Credit risk
Whether borrowers are likely to keep paying.
Analog
A past day whose five-light mix resembles today’s.
Base rate
What happened after those days. Not a forecast.
Credit impulse
The change in the flow of new credit — the second derivative, which turns before the level does. Central-bank plumbing is one cash story; private lending is the other.

Plumbing & rates

Asset (central bank)
Something the central bank owns — usually bonds, sometimes ETFs or loans.
Reserves
Cash banks hold at the Fed. Fuel for money markets.
TGA
Treasury General Account — the government’s checking account at the Fed. When it fills, cash leaves the banking system.
ON RRP
Overnight reverse repo — cash parked at the Fed overnight. When it rises, less cash sits in markets.
Net liquidity
Fed assets minus TGA minus ON RRP. Rising usually means more cash in markets.
Real yield
Yield after inflation (TIPS). Higher real yields tighten conditions for risk assets. The market’s true cost of money.
Term premium
The extra yield for holding a long bond rather than rolling short ones. Wide means duration is paid.
Breakeven
The inflation rate priced into markets from nominal bonds versus TIPS.
Curve (2s10s)
Long yield minus short yield. Negative (inverted) often warns of a slowdown.

Fear & credit

Investment grade
Higher-rated corporate bonds. Extra yield over Treasuries, but much of the risk is still duration — when yields rise, prices fall.
High yield
Lower-rated corporate bonds, also called junk. More about whether the borrower still pays; the spread is the wage for that risk.
OAS
Option-adjusted spread — extra yield a corporate bond pays over Treasuries, adjusted for embedded options. Wider means more credit stress.
VIX / MOVE
Implied volatility — equity fear (VIX) and Treasury fear (MOVE).
