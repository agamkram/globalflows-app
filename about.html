GlobalFlows

A live picture of how money and the real economy are moving together — cash, rates, growth, inflation, and fear — and what that mix usually means for bonds, credit, and risk assets.

The idea

Read left to right. Cheap or scarce cash shows up first. Rates and the dollar pass that pressure around. Growth and inflation say what households and firms are actually doing. Risk tells you whether markets are calm or stressed. When you know that mix, you can ask the useful question: so what for assets?

The five lights

Each colored light is a short status for one part of the machine — Liquidity, Rates, Growth, Inflation, or Risk. Behind every light is a handful of related public indicators. Together they decide whether that part of the machine looks easy, mixed, or tight.

  • Tap a light — the table jumps to the indicators that set it, highlighted in blue.
  • Tap “Tap for who voted” — see those indicators, their scores, and a short plain-language read.
  • 1w · 2w · 1m — the turn, not “is it high.” Lights stay on the economic level. One month is the default.

All five lights share one axis, and it is worth learning before anything else on this page. Green is the reflationary end — more cash, easier funding, firmer growth, hotter prices, more appetite for risk. Red is the contractionary end — the same five things going the other way. Neither colour means good or bad. A green Inflation light is not the app approving of inflation; it is telling you prices are running hot, which is usually bad for long bonds and fine for commodities. Read the colours as a direction the whole machine is leaning, then let the asset classes below tell you who that helps. Inflation votes core PCE, core CPI, and the market’s 5y5y — not household surveys. Risk votes VIX, high-yield and Baa spreads (the long credit history), and NFCI. MOVE stays on Rates as the bond-market stress gauge: a spike can tighten the light and the turn; calm does not vote easy.

Green — reflationary: easier, stronger, hotter, risk-on Amber — mixed, or near the middle Red — contractionary: tighter, softer, colder, risk-off

Why “global”

Dollars are made in more than one place. The Fed is the largest single source, but the European Central Bank, the Bank of Japan and China together run balance sheets of comparable size, and the money they create does not stay home — it competes for the same bonds, the same equities and the same credit. A dashboard that watches only the Fed will call liquidity tight while the rest of the world is flooding the system, and be wrong for years at a time.

So three of the voters are deliberately not American:

  • G4 balance sheets, 12-month change — the Fed, ECB, Bank of Japan and China added together in dollars, and measured by how fast the total is growing. A balance-sheet level rises forever and so can never be called high or low; the rate of change is the thing that eases or tightens. Note that flat is not neutral here: world nominal output grows around 5% a year, so balance sheets held still are quietly shrinking against the economy they fund.
  • The dollar, 12-month change — most of the world borrows in a currency it cannot print. When the dollar rises, every one of those borrowers is squeezed at once, no matter what any central bank intends. The dollar is the transmission belt, which is why it votes on Liquidity rather than just sitting on the tape.
  • Global long rates — the average 10-year yield across Germany, the UK and Japan, from official daily prints: Bundesbank Bund term structure, Bank of England gilt par yield, Japan Ministry of Finance JGB. Duration is priced globally: when Bunds and JGBs move, Treasuries follow. A Rates light built only from US yields mistakes a worldwide repricing for an American one. The OECD monthly averages stay on the book as the slow official print.

One honest limit. FRED does not carry the People's Bank of China balance sheet, so the China leg is Chinese FX reserves instead. That is not the same number — but for global dollar liquidity it is arguably the more relevant one, because reserves are the part that actually recycles into world markets. Euro area HICP and euro/Japan unemployment sit on the street and do not vote. The Growth and Inflation lights are still US voters. Japan monthly CPI is not on a live public pipe we can keep — OECD-on-FRED died in 2021 — so it is not faked.

So what

Under the five lights sit six asset classes — Treasuries (5 / 10 / 30), credit (investment grade / high yield), equities, crypto, gold, commodities. Green, amber, and red mean in, mixed, and out of favor. Tap a class for why that call, the curve or credit split, and the analog. Full regime inside that card is the longer duration / credit / confirm sheet.

Duration risk

How painful rising rates and sticky inflation are for long bonds and other assets that get paid far in the future. Rising means discount rates are biting harder.

Credit risk

How worried markets are that borrowers still pay. Built from growth, fear gauges, credit spreads, and whether bank lending is speeding up or slowing down.

In / out of favor

The same mix, mapped onto six asset classes. Treasuries split 5s, 10s, and 30s. Credit shows IG and HY; Equities shows Cy and Df; Commodity shows Oi and Cu. Crypto is the high-beta liquidity valve (Bitcoin). Gold is money/fear. Bills live on the Rates light, not as their own class.

Confirm

Extra checks that line up with the story — or don’t — such as how stocks and bonds are moving together, the dollar, gold, or bitcoin versus cash conditions.

Falsify

The clean flip that would undo the read. Example: inflation turns cold while growth stays strong softens a “duration is hard” call.

The mapping

Lights are where it is versus an economic line — 7% inflation is high even if it just fell. The 1w / 2w / 1m row is which way it’s going. The six asset classes use both. GDP is quarterly (1w and 1m share the latest quarter). Monthly prints have no true one-week turn — 1w and 1m share the same print. Liquidity color is scarce vs ample, not the dollar stock; the clocks show adding vs draining.

From the lights we score two risks. Duration asks whether long coupons are taxed (hot inflation, tight real funding) — cooling this window can soften that even when the level is still high. Credit asks whether borrowers still look good for the money. Stocks vs bonds follows that pair.

  • Treasuries — 5s follow policy, 10s follow duration and inflation, 30s follow inflation (and duration). A flight to quality can help 10s and 30s if inflation isn’t hot.
  • Credit — investment grade and high yield on the strip, like 5s / 10s / 30s. Investment-grade bonds care about duration as well as spreads; high yield cares about growth, fear, and whether the spread pays. Cycle-tight OAS takes high yield out even if fear is cheap. If the two disagree, the Credit title is mixed.
  • Equities — broad call when growth is firm and fear isn’t in charge. Soft growth, draining cash, or risk-off takes the title out. On the strip: Cy (cyclicals) wants Strong growth and calm fear; Df (defensives) wants Soft growth or expensive fear. Proxies are XLY and XLP.
  • Crypto — Bitcoin, the high-beta liquidity valve. In when plumbing is easy and fear isn’t in charge. Out when cash is draining or risk is off. It does not vote Liquidity; it confirms or falsifies it.
  • Gold — in for fear, a cash drain that isn’t a rates squeeze, or hot prices with easy funding. Hot inflation alone is not enough. Cold inflation, risk-on, and high funding usually leave it out.
  • Commodity — title from Growth and Inflation: in when both are firm/hot; out when growth is soft or prices are cold. On the strip: Oi (oil) follows Inflation; Cu (copper) follows Growth. They do not flip the title.

What happened last time

A read is worth more when you know how often it has been right. Under So what sits a base rate: the app scores every trading day back to 2018 with the same five lights, finds the days that sat closest to today, and shows what the market actually did over the weeks that followed — the median move and how often each asset rose. History agrees or disagrees only when both the hit rate and the median move beat the unconditional tape the same way.

Three things to hold in mind. It says how close the match is, and a distant match is context rather than evidence. Days sitting next to each other in the calendar are nearly the same day, so matches are kept at least a month apart to stop one episode counting as twenty. And the replay scores jobs, prices, GDP and NFCI on ALFRED vintages — the print that was public that morning. Funding, the curve, volatility and spreads are market-priced and not revised.

It is a base rate, not a forecast. Its most useful moments are when it disagrees with the read above it, because that tells you the call rests on this cycle being different from the last few.

How to read it

  1. Pick the lookback — 1w, 2w, or 1m for the turn. Lights stay on where it is now.
  2. Read the lights — cash → rates → growth → inflation → fear.
  3. Read the asset classes — what that mix usually favors, including 5 / 10 / 30.
  4. Open a light — the indicators behind it, and the short read.
  5. Tap an asset class — why that call, the split, the analog. Full regime is inside the card.
  6. Glance at Markets — last prints on open; stats from the daily pull.

Where the numbers come from

Public sources: FRED, the New York Fed, Yahoo Finance, Bundesbank, Bank of England, and Japan’s Ministry of Finance. A morning job bakes the full book. On open, last prints that can move — Yahoo tape, official dailies, G3 / SOFR spread / dollar 12m — refresh again. Weekly, monthly, and quarterly lines stay on the last official release. Analog history stays the morning snapshot.

Glossary

Reading the numbers

Level
Where a light sits versus an economic line (2% inflation, ~4% unemployment, VIX and high-yield bands). High is high. Not a table column — only a few series vote this.
Impulse
Up, flat, or down over 1w, 2w, or 1m. The turn. Monthly series: anything under about six weeks is the last print vs the one before.
1w · 2w · 1m
Lookback. Default 1m. Does not recolor the lights.

In the app

Lights
The five status colors for liquidity, rates, growth, inflation, and risk. Tap one to see the indicators behind it.
Markets
Stocks, commodities, crypto, gold, and related prices — how risk assets are behaving, separate from the five lights.
Last print
Newest print for series that can move between bakes. Age sits by the street title; the circle refreshes. Listed names can lag about 15 minutes. Chart uses the same last bar. Weekly/monthly/quarterly stay on the morning bake.
So what
The six asset classes under the lights, and the panel behind a tap: duration risk, credit risk, 5/10/30, IG/HY, Cy/Df, Oi/Cu on the strip, what confirms, and what would falsify.
The mapping
Usual in/out from the lights: level first, then the 1w/2w/1m lookback. Duration and credit, then the Treasury curve, credit, equities, crypto, gold, commodities. Crypto is the liquidity valve (Bitcoin) and does not vote a light. Gold needs a second job besides hot inflation — fear, a cash drain without a rates squeeze, or hot prices with easy funding.
Duration risk
How rate moves change the value of cash flows that arrive later (bonds, and stock valuations that lean on the far future).
Credit risk
Doubt that cash flows arrive at all — spreads, growth, and risk appetite.
Credit impulse
Whether bank lending growth is speeding up or slowing down. Central-bank plumbing is one cash story; private lending is the other.
Nominal − real GDP
Nominal growth minus real growth — how much of the expansion is prices versus actual activity.

Plumbing & rates

Asset (central bank)
Something the central bank owns — usually bonds, sometimes ETFs or loans.
Reserves
Cash banks hold at the Fed. Fuel for money markets.
TGA
Treasury General Account — the government’s checking account at the Fed. When it fills, cash leaves the banking system.
ON RRP
Overnight reverse repo — cash parked at the Fed overnight. When it rises, less cash sits in markets.
Net liquidity
Fed assets minus TGA minus ON RRP. Rising usually means more cash in markets.
Real yield
Yield after inflation (TIPS). Higher real yields tighten conditions for risk assets.
Breakeven
The inflation rate priced into markets from nominal bonds versus TIPS.
Curve (2s10s)
Long yield minus short yield. Negative (inverted) often warns of a slowdown.

Fear & credit

Investment grade
Higher-rated corporate bonds. Extra yield over Treasuries, but much of the risk is still duration — when yields rise, prices fall.
High yield
Lower-rated corporate bonds, also called junk. More about whether the borrower still pays; the spread is the wage for that risk.
OAS
Option-adjusted spread — extra yield a corporate bond pays over Treasuries. Wider means more credit stress.
VIX / MOVE
Implied volatility — equity fear (VIX) and Treasury fear (MOVE).
